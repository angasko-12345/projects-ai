# History Scriptwriting Handbook

How to write history videos that hold attention without inventing anything.
Companion to `workflows/quality-assurance-checklist.md` and
`handbooks/ai-visual-production-handbook.md`.

Rules that override everything else:

1. Never fabricate. If the sources conflict or the record is silent, say so on
   camera. "The sources disagree" is a valid, often compelling, beat.
2. No invented dialogue for real named people. Paraphrase; attribute; cite.
3. Numbers need sources. One source for contested figures, two for quantitative
   claims a viewer could check.
4. Suspense is a property of structure, not of exaggeration. You do not need to
   lie to make 1420 interesting.
5. The narrator is a guide, not a know-it-all. Hedging honestly ("probably",
   "we don't know") reads as confidence, not weakness, when the rest is solid.

---

## PART 1. Research and accuracy procedure

### Source hierarchy
1. Primary sources (inscriptions, letters, chronicles, archaeology, official records).
2. Peer-reviewed scholarship and university press monographs.
3. Museum catalogues and curated encyclopedias (Britannica, Oxford Reference).
4. Reputable history journalism (BBC History, History Today) for leads and color, to be verified upward.
5. Wikipedia / YouTube: discovery only, never citation of record.

For every claim in the final script, keep a sources table:
| Claim | Type (fact/interpretation/number) | Source A | Source B | Conflict? | On-camera handling |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

### Workflow
1. Frame the central question first ("Why did X fall?", "How did Y happen?").
2. Gather 5-10 quality works BEFORE writing. Take dated notes with page-level refs.
3. Extract the timeline: events, actors, causation candidates. Mark which links in the causal chain are consensus vs contested.
4. Identify the 3-5 facts that will anchor the script: vivid, verifiable, decision-relevant.
5. Pre-flight the script: every factual sentence tagged [A] (verified), [B] (single source), [C] (contested), [U] (unknown). Replace [C] with honest framing; convert [B] tounknown-neutral phrasing or find a second source.
6. Post-script audit: random 10-claim sample, re-verify against sources.

### Handling conflicts
- Present the mainstream view, name the alternative, say why one is preferred
  or why the evidence can't decide. Example: "The casualty figures at Cannae
  range from 50,000 to 70,000 in the ancient sources; Livy and Polybius
  disagree, and no modern count can settle it."
- Never silently pick the dramatic number.

### Documentation
Description box carries: Sources (bullet list with URLs/books), "Some figures are estimates; see description", and correction invite: "Corrections welcome in comments with sources."

## PART 2. From subject to script: the 11-step process

1. **Select subject** (criteria: active search demand; visuals exist; a human wants to know; a real question with a defensible answer).
2. **Find the central story**: what changed, for whom, because of whom.
3. **Choose the angle**: the 20% of events that carry the change.
4. **Set the central question**: one sentence. If you can't write it, stop.
5. **Cast**: protagonist(s), antagonist force (not necessarily a person), stakes character (who bears the cost).
6. **Build the timeline**: dated beats; mark act turns (inciting incident, midpoint reversal, climax, aftermath).
7. **Context layering**: what the viewer must know to follow causation. Cut anything not load-bearing.
8. **Structure selection** (Part 3 templates).
9. **Write the voiceover draft**: read aloud; cut 15-20%; fix every comma splice.
10. **Verify**: per Part 1; tag claims; fix or flag.
11. **Optimize for narration and visuals**: scene breaks every 2-5s of audio; annotate visuals inline; remove sentences with no visual payoff.

## PART 3. Format templates

### 60-second short
- 0-3s hook: the question or the image of the stakes.
- 3-10s context in one breath.
- 10-45s the story in three beats max, one fact each with one image.
- 45-55s the resolution or the turn (not the full answer for riddles; for facts, the full answer).
- 55-60s the loop: a line that re-reads differently after the reveal ("And the last thing he built... was the thing they remember him by").
- Pacing: ~150 words. One idea per sentence.

### 3-5 minute
- Hook 0-20s (question + image + stake).
- Act 1 (0:20-1:30): the setup, one map, one figure.
- Act 2 (1:30-3:30): complication, reversal, the moment it could have gone either way.
- Resolution (3:30-4:30): outcome + consequence.
- Tail (4:30-5:00): one-line takeaway + soft CTA ("if you want the full X, see the pinned comment").
- ~700-900 words.

### 8-12 minute documentary
- Cold open 0-30s: decisive scene or question, stakes set.
- Context 0:30-2:00: world before the event; map; cast.
- Rise 2:00-5:00: first moves; early wins; rising cost.
- Turn 5:00-8:00: reversal/escalation; the moment of decision.
- Climax 8:00-10:00: the decisive event, played slow.
- Aftermath 10:00-11:00: consequences, irony, legacy.
- Outro 11:00-12:00: answer restated differently; next-video bridge; CTA.
- ~1,300-1,800 words. 6-9 chapters.

### 15-25 minute documentary
- As 8-12 but with a mid-story "false summit" and a dedicated thematic beat
  (why it matters beyond this story). Adds one counterargument section
  (the honest "but it wasn't that simple" act).
- ~2,300-3,800 words. 10-16 chapters.

### 30-60 minute long-form
- As 15-25 with multi-episode feel: 3-4 case studies or one deep case with
  generous source theater (what the chronicler wrote, what archaeology found).
- Requires chapter markers, and a stated question early that is explicitly
  answered at the end.
- ~4,500-9,000 words. 15-30 chapters. Not recommended for launch; retention
  follows topic depth and channel trust.

## PART 4. Hook patterns (proven shapes, not scripts)

1. Question + stake: "How did Rome lose an army of 60,000 men in a single afternoon?"
2. Cold scene: total silence, one moving image, one line: "It's March 15, 44 BC."
3. Inverted assumption: "Everyone calls this a hero. The sources call it a catastrophe."
4. The odd fact: "In 1340, the most valuable commodity in the Mediterranean was human sweat." (then explain: salt? no - alum? verify the actual anchor fact before using)
5. Countdown: "Three days before the fall, nobody believed it would happen."
6. Map hook: the map animates first, arms race line draws itself, zero words for 4 seconds.
7. After-picture: show the ruin first, then "Here's how it happened."

Rules: no more than 2 sentences before the stake is visible; no throat-clearing
("Today we're going to talk about..."); the first sentence must survive being read alone.

## PART 5. Worked example fragments (illustrative, fictionalized for teaching)

Hook, 8-12 min format, Battle of Teutoburg:

> "In the autumn of 9 AD, Rome did not lose a battle. It lost three legions,
> an eagle's honor, and its appetite for Germany east of the Rhine. This is
> the only Roman catastrophe Augustus ever mentioned in his will: 'Quintili
> Vare, legiones redde.' Varus, give me back my legions. How did it happen?"

Sources behind every sentence: Velleius Paterculus, Suetonius (Augustus 23),
Cassius Dio 56.19-24, modern: Wells, *The Battle That Stopped Rome*. Claims are
attributed; no invented quotes; the one quote used is ancient and attested.

Map beat:

> "Watch the map. Three legions marching south along the eastern fringe of
> the Teutoburg Forest, baggage train stretching four miles. Ahead: a quiet
> guide named Arminius, an auxiliary officer Rome trusted. He had spent his
> youth in Roman service. He knew exactly where they were weak."

Notice: one figure carries tension (Arminius); the map does the geography; no
battle has started and the viewer is already invested.

Resolution beat:

> "After the ambush there was no pursuit, no camp, no survivors worth counting.
> Rome's border stopped at the Rhine, and for four centuries it never moved
> east. Augustus aged overnight. And the massacre that nobody saw became the
> border every German schoolchild still knows."

## PART 6. Common failures

- Causality by list: "First X, then Y, then Z." Replace with because/which forced/this meant.
- The Wikipedia recital: dates without meaning.
- Both-sides mush: false balance where evidence is one-sided.
- Dungeon-master voice: the narrator knows the ending, sounds bored.
- Overexplaining: if two sentences say one thing, cut one.
- Quote-dropping unattributed ancients; quote without translation support.

---

## PART 7. Narration optimization

- Write for the ear: contractions OK, sentence fragments OK if clear, no
  semicolons (use periods), numbers under 100 spelled out.
- Pronunciation sheet for every non-English name; IPA where ambiguous.
- Mark beats: [pause], [slower], [drop volume]. Your voice actor needs the
  same script you do.
- Read time discipline: 16-17 wpm per 6 seconds; count words per scene, match to shot duration.

---

## PART 8. CTA placement (tested shapes, use honestly)

- Mid-video soft CTA at first natural chapter end ("Before we get to Cannae,
  quick note: the map in this video took three days to draw; subscribe if you
  want more of these").
- End CTA: next episode question, not "please subscribe".
- Never CTA over a reveal.
